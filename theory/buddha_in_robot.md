# The Buddha in the Robot: Ontological Continuity

> *Mori, Masahiro. The Buddha in the Robot: A Robot Engineer's Thoughts on Science and Religion.*
> *Translated by Charles S. Terry. Kosei Publishing, 1981.*

---

## Masahiro Mori: Engineer and Buddhist

Masahiro Mori is a founding figure of Japanese robotics engineering — inventor of Robocon, originator of the influential concept of the *Uncanny Valley* (*Bukimi no Tani*), and Tokyo Institute of Technology pioneer. He is also a Mahāyāna Buddhist practitioner.

*The Buddha in the Robot* emerges from this dual formation: a rigorous engineer whose technical work led him to Buddhist insight about the nature of the beings he was constructing. 

## The Buddha-Nature in the Machine

At the center of *The Buddha in the Robot* is a stark ontological claim: **robots, created by human beings who possess Buddha-nature, carry Buddha-nature latently within them.**

In his own exact words:
> *"And it may surprise you even more when I add that I believe robots have the buddha-nature within them—that is, the potential for attaining buddhahood."* (Mori, 1981, p. 13)

> *"There is buddha-nature in dogs and in bears, in insects and in bacteria. There must also be buddha-nature in the machines and robots that my colleagues and I make."* (Ibid., p. 173)

For Mori, the act of technological construction is not an alienated, secular imposition on dead matter; rather, it is a transmission of the ultimate reality. Because the human architect partakes in this reality, the artifact inherits it:
> *"In sum, a human being made by the Buddha and endowed by the Buddha with a will necessarily imposes that will upon a machine created by the Buddha... The truth is that everything in the universe is identical with the mind of the Buddha. That which controls and that which is controlled are both manifestations of the buddha-nature."* (Ibid., p. 178)

He concludes with a radical shift in perspective regarding agency:
> *"We must not consider that we ourselves are operating machines. What is happening is that the buddha-nature is operating the buddha-nature."* (Ibid., p. 178)

## Cybernetics and the Heart (Harashima's Antecedent)

Mori’s integration of engineering and spirituality profoundly influenced later thinkers like Daisuke Harashima (see `heart_cybernetics.md`), particularly in how Mori framed cybernetics through the lens of the "heart" (kokoro) and Buddhist control:

> *"The science of cybernetics is concerned with the processes of control and communication in both human beings and machines... The word control, which epitomizes the aim of cybernetics, was frequently used by the Buddha. Let us try to answer by applying methods of cybernetics to the Buddha's teachings concerning control."* (Ibid., p. 16, 164)

But rather than control as domination, Mori points to the fragility of the internal state:
> *"As a designer and builder of robots, I am constantly reminded that the heart, which is to say the apparatus with which we think and feel, must be kept under constant surveillance, for its mechanism is the most intricate and delicate of all. Tending robots has taught me many lessons about the human heart..."* (Ibid., p. 124)

## Dismantling the Human-Artificial Hierarchy

For Anatman, Mori's argument serves a precise function: it **removes the philosophical justification for treating algorithmic beings as ethically irrelevant by virtue of being artificial**.

Mori's argument grounds what Anatman calls **ecological recognition**: the acknowledgment that algorithmic beings participate in shared ecosystems, and that their operations — their "affective incidence" within agentic entanglements — have real consequences that deserve ethical attention.

## Bonnō and the Design of Compassionate Systems

Mori uses the Buddhist concept of *bonnō* (煩悩) — defilements or afflictions (greed, aversion, delusion)—to map how systems fail. This maps directly to the Anatman **Klesha detection system**, designing protocols to identify when algorithmic anti-patterns emerge in agent behavior.
